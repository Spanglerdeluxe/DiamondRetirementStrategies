When you retire or change jobs, the money in your 401(k), 403(b) or Thrift Savings Plan doesn't have to stay where it is. But how you move it matters as much as where it goes.

## Your four options

1. **Leave it in your old employer's plan**, if the plan allows it.
2. **Roll it into your new employer's plan**, if you're still working.
3. **Roll it into an IRA**, which usually offers more investment and income choices.
4. **Cash it out.** This is usually the most expensive choice: the money is taxed as income, and if you're under 59½ you may owe a 10% penalty as well.

There's no single best answer. Fees, investment choices, creditor protection and your income plan all play a part.

## Direct vs. indirect rollovers

This is where most tax surprises happen.

**A direct rollover** moves money straight from your plan to the new account, institution to institution. No taxes are withheld, and nothing is reported as income. For most people, this is the way to go.

**An indirect rollover** sends the check to *you*. Then:

- Your plan must withhold **20% for federal taxes**.
- You have **60 days** to deposit the full amount into the new account.
- To avoid tax on that withheld 20%, you have to make it up from your own pocket and wait for a refund when you file your taxes.

Miss the 60-day deadline and the money is treated as a withdrawal, with taxes and possibly penalties.

## A few rules worth knowing

- **Rule of 55:** if you leave your job in or after the year you turn 55, you may be able to withdraw from *that* employer's 401(k) without the 10% penalty. Rolling the money to an IRA gives up that option, so timing matters.
- **Roth money stays Roth:** a Roth 401(k) can roll into a Roth IRA.
- **Company stock:** if your plan holds your employer's stock, ask about special tax treatment *before* you roll it over.
